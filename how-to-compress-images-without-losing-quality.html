How to Compress Images Without Losing Quality

Published August 2026 · 7 min read

"Compress without losing quality" sounds like a contradiction — any lossy compression technically discards some information. But in practice, there's a wide, very usable range of settings where the discarded information is completely invisible to the human eye while the file size drops dramatically. The real goal isn't zero loss; it's loss you'll never notice.

Format decides more than any slider does

Before you touch a quality setting, pick the right container for the job. Photographs full of gradients, textures, and natural color variation — portraits, landscapes, product shots — compress beautifully as JPEG or WEBP. Graphics with flat colors, sharp text, or transparency — logos, icons, screenshots — belong in PNG. Run a screenshot through JPEG compression and you'll see exactly why: text edges start to smear and colored fringing appears around sharp lines, because JPEG's algorithm assumes photographic noise that simply isn't there.

Stay in the 70–85% quality range

JPEG and WEBP encoders expose a quality value, usually from 0–100. Push it above roughly 85% and you're paying a steep file-size cost for a difference nobody will see. Drop below about 60% and blocky artifacts start showing up around edges and in flat areas like skies or skin. A setting between 70 and 85% is the sweet spot for the overwhelming majority of photos, typically cutting file size by 60–90% versus an uncompressed original with zero visible difference at normal viewing size.

Resize before you compress, not after

A 4000×3000px photo destined for a web page that displays images at 1200px wide is carrying nearly nine times more pixel data than it needs. Resizing to the image's actual display dimensions before compressing very often saves more space than any amount of quality-slider tweaking — and costs you nothing visually, since a browser was going to throw away those extra pixels anyway. A useful rule of thumb: size your source image to about 1.5–2x its maximum display width, which covers high-density "retina" screens without carrying pixels nobody will see.

Watch out for repeated re-compression

Every time a JPEG gets opened, edited, and re-saved as JPEG, it loses a little more — the compression artifacts compound with each pass. If an image will go through multiple rounds of editing, keep a lossless master (PNG, or your editor's native format) and only export to JPEG once, at the very end, for final delivery.

Always preview before you commit

A number on a slider doesn't tell you what an image actually looks like. Zoom into a detailed area — an eye, a line of text, a busy texture — in the compressed result before locking in a setting. This is exactly the workflow Squish is built around: drop in an image, adjust quality and size, and see the compressed result immediately in its own card next to the original, so you can judge quality with your eyes instead of guessing from a percentage.

A quick decision checklist

  • Photo with gradients or lots of color → JPEG or WEBP, quality 70–85%.
  • Graphic, logo, icon, or screenshot with text → PNG.
  • Resize to the image's real display dimensions before compressing.
  • Keep an uncompressed master if the image will be re-edited later.
  • Always preview the compressed result before downloading.

Get these five habits right and "smaller file, same quality" stops being a trade-off you have to think hard about — it just becomes how you export images by default.
