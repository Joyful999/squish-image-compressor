How to Compress Images for Faster Website Loading

Published August 2026 · 6 min read

Images are usually the single heaviest part of any web page — frequently outweighing every stylesheet, script, and font combined. That makes image compression one of the highest-leverage changes available for page speed, and one of the few that doesn't require touching a line of code.

Why this affects more than just "loading fast"

Google's Core Web Vitals include Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) — a measurement of how long it takes the largest visible element on a page to render, which is very often a hero image or featured photo. An oversized, uncompressed image directly slows this metric down, and a poor LCP score can affect search ranking as well as user experience. Shrinking that one image is frequently the single fastest way to improve it.

Find your heaviest images first

Open your browser's developer tools on your slowest page, switch to the Network tab, and sort requests by size. It's extremely common to find one hero image or background photo sitting at several megabytes — often larger than every other resource on the page put together. That's your highest-priority target.

Compress with a quality setting that holds up

Once you've resized an image to its real display dimensions (see our guide on reducing image size for websites), apply JPEG or WEBP compression at roughly 75–85% quality. Combined correctly, resizing and compression commonly cut total image weight by 70–90% with no visible difference to a visitor — which translates directly into a faster-rendering page.

Don't stop at the hero image

Every image on a page contributes to total page weight, even ones that load lazily further down. A gallery of ten uncompressed thumbnails can easily add up to more total weight than one oversized hero banner. Compress your entire image library as a habit, not just the single largest file you happened to notice.

Batch the work instead of doing it one file at a time

If a page — or an entire site migration — involves dozens of images, compressing them one at a time in an editor is a slow, repetitive process. Squish lets you drop in a whole batch, apply one set of quality and resize settings to everything at once, and download every compressed file together as a ZIP — turning an afternoon of individual exports into a couple of minutes. Open the compressor and run your next batch of page images through it before you upload them.

A repeatable performance workflow

  1. Audit your heaviest pages using browser dev tools.
  2. Resize each oversized image to its real display width.
  3. Compress at 75–85% quality, using WEBP or JPEG for photos.
  4. Re-check total page weight and move to the next-heaviest page.

None of this requires a developer, a plugin, or a hosting upgrade — just a habit of compressing images correctly before they ever reach your site.
