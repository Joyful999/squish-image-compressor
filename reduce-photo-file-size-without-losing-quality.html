How to Reduce Photo File Size Without Losing Quality

Published August 2026 · 6 min read

Modern phone cameras routinely produce 4–12MB photos — far larger than almost any real-world use actually needs. Whether you're clearing up storage, preparing photos to share, or getting ready to email a batch to family, there's a reliable way to shrink those files dramatically without anyone noticing a quality difference.

Resolution and quality are two different levers

It helps to separate two ideas that often get lumped together. Resolution is the pixel dimensions of the photo — how much detail is physically captured. Quality (in the JPEG/WEBP sense) is how efficiently that detail gets compressed into a file. You can lower quality without touching resolution, lower resolution without touching quality, or adjust both together — and for most everyday photos, a combination of the two gets you the biggest size reduction with the least visible change.

How much resolution do you actually need?

Unless a photo is headed for large-format printing or heavy cropping later, full camera resolution is usually overkill:

  • Sharing on a phone or social media: 1600–2048px on the longest side is plenty.
  • Standard printing up to roughly 8×10 inches: around 2400px on the longest side.
  • Large prints or heavy cropping: keep full camera resolution.

Resizing down to the resolution you actually need, before compressing, is often the single biggest space-saver available — sometimes cutting file size by 70% or more before quality settings even come into play.

Quality settings for photographic content

Camera and phone photos are usually detailed, natural images — exactly the kind of content JPEG and WEBP compress well. A quality setting between 70 and 85% is very hard to distinguish from the original at normal viewing sizes, while cutting file size dramatically. For the technical reasoning behind that range, see our guide on compressing images without losing quality.

Doing this for a whole photo library at once

If you're clearing space on a phone or hard drive, compressing photos one at a time isn't realistic. Batch processing — applying the same resize and quality settings to an entire folder in one pass — turns hours of manual exporting into a couple of minutes. Squish supports exactly this: drop in as many photos as you like, set your target size and quality once, and download every compressed file together as a ZIP. Try compressing a batch of your own photos and compare the before-and-after file sizes for yourself.

What to keep as a backup

Compression is one-directional — once detail is discarded, it can't be recovered. If there's any chance you'll want to print a photo large or edit it heavily later, keep the original file somewhere safe (an external drive, cloud backup, or archive folder) and only compress a copy for the specific purpose at hand — sharing, emailing, or everyday storage.
