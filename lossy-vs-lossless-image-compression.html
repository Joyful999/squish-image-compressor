Lossy vs Lossless Image Compression: What's the Difference?

Published August 2026 · 6 min read

Every image compressor makes a fundamental choice before a single pixel gets touched: whether to discard some information permanently in exchange for a much smaller file, or to preserve every pixel exactly and accept a larger one. That choice is the difference between lossy and lossless compression, and understanding it makes every other format decision easier.

Lossy compression: smaller files, selective detail loss

Lossy compression — the approach JPEG and lossy WEBP use — analyzes an image and discards the detail human eyes are least likely to notice, then encodes what's left far more efficiently than a lossless method could. The result is a dramatically smaller file, often 80–95% smaller than an uncompressed original, at a quality level most people genuinely cannot distinguish from the source. The catch: information really is gone. Compress the same image repeatedly and the losses compound, gradually introducing visible artifacts.

Lossless compression: every pixel, exactly

Lossless compression — the approach PNG and lossless WEBP use — finds ways to represent the exact same pixel data more efficiently, without discarding anything. Decompress a lossless file and you get back pixel-for-pixel the same image that went in. This is essential for content where exactness matters: text, line art, technical diagrams, and anything that might be edited or re-compressed repeatedly. The trade-off is size — lossless files are generally larger than lossy ones, sometimes dramatically so for photographic content.

Why photos favor lossy and graphics favor lossless

Photographs contain huge amounts of natural, chaotic detail — subtle color variation, sensor noise, complex textures — much of which is genuinely imperceptible once compressed. Lossy compression exploits exactly this, which is why it works so well on photos and so poorly on flat graphics: there's no "imperceptible noise" to discard in a solid-color logo or a line of text, so lossy compression on that content mostly just introduces visible artifacts instead of hiding losses in noise the eye already ignores.

A practical way to choose

  • Choose lossy (JPEG, lossy WEBP) when the image is a photo, file size matters more than pixel-perfect exactness, and the content has natural gradients or texture to hide compression in.
  • Choose lossless (PNG, lossless WEBP) when the image contains text, sharp edges, transparency, or will be edited and re-saved multiple times.

Lossy doesn't mean "low quality"

It's a common misconception that lossy compression automatically means a visibly worse image. In reality, a well-chosen lossy setting — typically 70–85% quality for JPEG or WEBP — produces a file that looks identical to the uncompressed original at normal viewing sizes while being a fraction of the size. The quality loss only becomes visible when the setting is pushed too aggressively, which is exactly what a good compression tool helps you avoid by showing a live preview as you adjust it.
